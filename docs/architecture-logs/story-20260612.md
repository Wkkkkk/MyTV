# What the Lens Carries

_A reflection on the 2026-06-12 architecture-deepening effort, and on the toolkit behind it._
_Companion to the proposal it looks back on: [changes-20260612.md](changes-20260612.md)._

---

We finished a campaign, and then we paused to look at the tool that made it possible.

The campaign itself is recorded in [changes-20260612.md](changes-20260612.md): five shallow
modules turned deep, ranked and shipped. What this note is about is the thing we noticed
*after* — that the lens we'd been swinging so confidently, *turn a shallow module into a deep
one*, was never a single idea. It's a small, sharp interface with a great deal of inherited
machinery behind it. A deep module in its own right.

The core lens is Ousterhout's, from *A Philosophy of Software Design*: a module is **deep**
when a lot of behavior sits behind a small interface, **shallow** when the interface is
nearly as complex as the implementation. That much we already knew we were using. The
reflection was in everything *around* it — the cluster of ideas the
`improve-codebase-architecture` skill carries beyond the headline. They sort into four kinds:
diagnostics, seam discipline, a testing doctrine, and some deliberate refinements of the
skill's own sources.

---

## 1. Diagnostics — how to *spot* shallowness

The lens tells you what deep and shallow *are*; the diagnostics tell you how to find the
shallow thing in front of you.

- **The deletion test.** Imagine deleting the module. If complexity *vanishes*, it was a
  pass-through and you've lost nothing. If complexity *reappears across N callers*, it was
  earning its keep. This is the skill's primary instrument, and in retrospect it was the
  quiet judge behind all five of our picks — candidate #1's intake validation was the
  clearest "delete it and the rules reappear, copied, in every form and JSON handler."

- **Friction-first exploration.** Rather than rigid heuristics, the skill says to notice
  *where you experience friction*: needing to bounce between many small modules to understand
  one concept (Ousterhout's "classitis," unnamed here), or pure functions extracted *only*
  for testability while the real bugs hide in how they're *called*. That second pattern is
  exactly what candidate #3 was — the conversion's logic had been split into testable pieces,
  but the failure path lived in the `tokio::spawn` that called them, where no test could
  reach. Extraction without locality is a smell, not a fix.

## 2. Seam discipline — where to *place* the cut

A seam, in the skill's borrowed vocabulary, is **Michael Feathers'**: *a place where you can
alter behavior without editing in that place*. Choosing where it goes is its own decision,
separate from what sits behind it. The discipline is about not cutting carelessly.

- **One adapter is a hypothetical seam; two adapters is a real one.** Don't introduce a seam
  unless something actually varies across it — typically production plus test. A
  single-adapter seam is just indirection wearing a costume. This is the skill's guard
  against the over-abstraction that the deep-module lens, taken naively, could encourage.

- **Internal seams versus external seams.** A deep module can be *internally* composed of
  small, swappable parts used by its own tests, without exposing them through its interface.
  Candidate #3 is the textbook case: the yt-dlp resolver is injected as a closure — a real,
  load-bearing *internal* seam the tests drive — yet `convert_if_ended`'s *external*
  interface stayed small. The seam that serves the tests is not the same as the seam the
  callers see, and the skill is explicit that you shouldn't conflate them.

## 3. A testing doctrine

This is where the skill is most opinionated, and where our effort drew the most from it.

- **The interface is the test surface.** Callers and tests cross the *same* seam. If you find
  yourself wanting to test *past* the interface, the module is the wrong shape. This was the
  literal through-line of the campaign: four of five candidates existed to stop tests reaching
  past a seam. Candidate #4 manufactured a pure `build_proxy_headers` so the RFC header rules
  could be tested without a live server; #3 made the conversion awaitable so a test could
  observe it instead of bypassing it.

- **Replace, don't layer.** Once tests exist at the deepened interface, the old unit tests on
  the modules it absorbed become *waste* — delete them, don't keep both. Depth that leaves a
  layer of stale tests behind hasn't finished the job.

- **Tests assert observable outcomes, not internal state**, so they survive internal
  refactors. A test that has to change when the implementation changes was testing past the
  interface all along.

- **A dependency taxonomy that decides the testing strategy.** The skill sorts a candidate's
  dependencies into four kinds — *in-process*, *local-substitutable*, *remote-but-owned*
  (ports & adapters), *true-external* (mock) — and the category dictates how you test across
  the seam. All five of our candidates were category one, pure in-process; that is *why* none
  of them needed a port, and naming the category made that an explicit decision rather than a
  lucky accident.

## 4. Deliberate refinements of its sources

The most interesting discovery was that the skill doesn't merely quote its sources — it
*edits* them.

- **It rejects Ousterhout's own metric.** Ousterhout defines depth as the ratio of
  implementation lines to interface lines. The skill throws that out — it "rewards padding the
  implementation" — and substitutes **depth-as-leverage**: behavior exercised per unit of
  interface a caller or test must learn. Same word, better metric. This matters: by the
  line-ratio metric, candidate #5 (a tiny row builder) would look like a poor deepening; by
  the leverage metric it's correctly judged *Speculative but real* — small mass, genuine
  locality win.

- **It splits the payoff in two.** *Leverage* is what callers get from depth — capability per
  unit of interface learned. *Locality* is what maintainers get — change, bugs, and knowledge
  concentrated in one place, "fix once, fixed everywhere." The skill insists every benefit be
  argued in *those* terms, not in vague appeals to "cleaner code." Our five specs obeyed this
  almost to a fault.

- **Design it twice.** Also Ousterhout, also carried forward: your first interface is
  unlikely to be your best, so the skill is willing to spawn parallel agents under opposing
  constraints — minimize the interface, maximize flexibility, optimize the common caller — and
  compare. We never invoked this in the campaign, but it's the escape hatch for when the right
  shape isn't obvious.

- **Language discipline as a feature, not a style note.** Domain nouns come from the project's
  own vocabulary; architecture terms come from the skill's `LANGUAGE.md`; and "component,"
  "service," "API," and "boundary" are banned outright ("boundary" because DDD already owns
  it). Consistent language is stated as *the whole point* — the same conviction that makes a
  deep module worth building in the first place.

---

## The reflection

Stand back and the shape is this: we used a lens to do real work, and then we turned the lens
around to read its own construction. What we found was that *turn a shallow module into a deep
one* is itself a fusion — Ousterhout's depth, Feathers' seams, a hexagonal testing taxonomy,
and a handful of opinionated refinements — packaged behind a four-word interface. The skill
practices on itself the thing it preaches.

The practical lesson for this codebase is small but durable. The next time we reach for "make
this deeper," the honest question isn't only *is the interface smaller?* It's the cluster:
*Does the deletion test pass? Is there a second adapter, or am I building indirection? Will
the interface be the test surface, or will tests still reach past it? And can I argue the win
in leverage and locality — not lines?* That checklist, not the slogan, is what actually
shipped the five.
